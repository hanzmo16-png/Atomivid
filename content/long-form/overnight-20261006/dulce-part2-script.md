# DULCE Part II: Nightmare Hall

English editorial draft. Not narrated, not rendered, not publication-approved.
All underground scenes are explicitly fictionalized reconstructions of unverified claims, not newly discovered evidence. Source review and continuity audit remain required before paid assets.

## 1 — The floor below

The elevator stops. Beyond its walls, a machine keeps turning. Somewhere down the corridor, a light changes from white to red. In the story attributed to Thomas Castello, this was the point where a strange job became something far worse. He had helped protect the building. Now he feared what the building was protecting.

Last time, we followed that account to a decision: keep obeying the rules, or investigate the lower levels. Tonight, we follow the legend into the place its tellers call Nightmare Hall. The corridor you see is a dramatization. We do not have authenticated footage of this alleged facility, or independent confirmation of the experiences attributed to its supposed guard.

That distinction will matter at the end. Because there are two mysteries here. What did the story say was behind the glass? And how did a story without a verified witness acquire the atmosphere of a classified case? First, the descent. Then, the evidence. Stay with both, because the most disturbing turn may arrive after we leave the underground rooms behind.

## 2 — Behind the glass

Published versions of the Castello account describe restricted research areas, containment, and people allegedly held without consent. Some versions attach the name Nightmare Hall to the sixth level. These are claims within the legend. A numbered floor plan is not proof that such a building exists.

For this reconstruction, imagine an observation gallery. On one side, ordinary instruments. On the other, shapes behind frosted glass. A technician checks a display without looking up. A security worker waits for an instruction that never comes. None of those individual actions is recovered testimony; they are the visual language of our dramatization.

The fear comes from the arrangement. A room can look clean and still suggest something cruel. A procedure can look routine and still leave the person undergoing it powerless. That is the nightmare this story asks its audience to imagine: an institution so accustomed to crossing a boundary that nobody inside pauses to name it.

But notice what our imagination has already supplied. A sound. A face. A fluorescent light. The moment we picture those details, an allegation starts feeling like a memory. It is not our memory. And a convincing picture cannot give it a chain of evidence.

## 3 — The person in the room

Part One introduced George, the man who, in the account we reconstructed, said he had been brought there against his will. His role changes the emotional stakes. A secret machine is a puzzle. A person who cannot leave is a moral emergency. That difference is why this part of the legend is so powerful.

In our dramatized scene, the guard is outside the room. The seated man is inside it. We do not need to show an operation, a wound, or a monster to understand the division. One person can walk away. The other may not be able to. The camera stays with that distance rather than pretending to document an atrocity.

Now imagine returning home after believing you had witnessed something like this. The table is familiar. Someone asks about your day. You begin an answer, then abandon it. This is an imagined emotional continuation of the secrecy explored in Part One, not a quotation from a verified conversation with Castello's family.

What makes the silence frightening is its ambiguity. Is the man protecting his loved ones? Protecting himself? Protecting an employer? Or are we watching a fictional device that makes an otherwise unverified narrator feel human? The feeling is real. The biography remains a separate question.

## 4 — The breaking point

The wider Dulce legend escalates from hidden research to conflict inside the alleged installation. Different retellings add different participants and explanations. We cannot reconstruct an exact battle from that material. There is no independently established casualty list or authenticated incident record in the source material reviewed for this episode.

So the sequence you see is a visualization of the legend's turning point, not a claim that we know how an underground confrontation unfolded. An alarm washes the corridor red. Staff move away from an observation area. A security figure hesitates between two passages. We cut before an imagined firefight can masquerade as historical reporting.

The dramatic question is whether someone who helped enforce the rules can turn against them. The investigative question is less cinematic, but more important: who first told this part, when did they tell it, and what can another person verify without relying on the same storyteller?

We should also resist combining every famous underground-base account into a single event. Similar settings do not establish shared witnesses. Repeated details may reveal borrowing rather than corroboration. A stronger ending to the legend would be an escape. A stronger beginning to an investigation would be a document whose origin we can actually establish.

## 5 — A name is not a witness

Put the corridors aside. What would it take to verify the account? A traceable interview recording. An identifiable interviewer. Employment records that can be checked independently. Contemporaneous material whose custody is documented. These are examples of evidence we would seek, not items we are claiming to possess.

The material reviewed here does not establish the alleged security worker's identity or employment. That does not allow us to prove that every person with the name was invented. It means the attribution is not enough to carry the extraordinary conclusions attached to it. The burden belongs to the claim.

Consider an illustration repeated on five websites. There are five pages, but perhaps only one underlying source. Now imagine the same unverified interview translated, summarized, and narrated by several channels. The number of retellings grows. The number of independent witnesses may remain unchanged.

This is why our own images need labels. If a frame from this episode appears elsewhere without context, it could become another apparent exhibit in the same cycle. The facility, the figures behind glass, and the alarm sequence were made to illustrate a story. They cannot be used to authenticate it. The question changes from what looks persuasive to what survives an independent check.

## 6 — The trail of disinformation

Part One also introduced Paul Bennewitz as a separate thread in the wider Dulce discussion. We must not treat his name as a bridge that automatically verifies the Castello story. His story needs to be investigated on its own terms. Placing two names side by side does not establish that their experiences were connected.

The filmmakers behind Mirage Men offer a different perspective on UFO narratives. On their own project website, they describe investigating Richard Doty and allegations that disinformation was introduced into UFO communities around sensitive military activity. This is their account of an investigation, not an official finding that settles every claim about Dulce.

It introduces a possibility worth examining carefully: an extraordinary explanation can distract from an ordinary secret. That possibility is not permission to attribute every rumor to a coordinated operation. Proving a deception also requires evidence. Skepticism should apply to a dramatic debunking as firmly as it applies to a dramatic allegation.

Imagine a desk with two folders. One concerns stories about an underground installation. The other concerns claims about manipulation of belief. Laying them next to each other makes a compelling image. It does not, by itself, establish that one caused the other. The missing link cannot be replaced by music, editing, or our desire for a perfect twist.

## 7 — What remains after the descent

We entered Nightmare Hall through a story. We leave it with a different set of questions. Who can be identified? Which documents can be traced? Which details have an independent origin? What changes from one version to the next? Those questions may feel smaller than an underground battle. They are the ones that could move an investigation forward.

Nothing in this reconstruction establishes an alien laboratory beneath Dulce. Nor does an absence of verification authorize us to invent a complete explanation for how every part of the legend began. The honest boundary is narrower: the claims remain unverified, and the images you have watched are artistic reconstructions.

There is still something unsettling here. We can feel fear in a room we have never entered. We can recognize a face that was created for the screen. We can remember an invented alarm more vividly than a careful qualification. A mystery documentary has to account for that power, especially when its pictures are designed to look real.

Perhaps the lasting question is not how far down the elevator goes. It is how far a story can travel before someone asks where it started. If you have an original document or a traceable source, share its provenance, not just its conclusion.
