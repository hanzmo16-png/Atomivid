# Video #004 — Research packet: Thermopylae, 480 BC

Every statement is tagged FACT (documented in the cited source), INFERENCE (reasonable interpretation, stated as
such in narration), CONTESTED (sources disagree; narration says so) or VISUAL RECONSTRUCTION (what we depict;
labelled as reconstruction in the shot contract, never asserted as footage). Primary source throughout:
Herodotus, *Histories* Book 7 (cited as Hdt 7.xxx; Perseus/Godley text and the Macaulay translation were used).
Rows were checked against the sources on 2026-10-01 (see VERIFICATION.md). A human re-read of Herodotus 7.175–239
and of the modern works cited is required before recording. Nothing in this packet is drawn from any earlier
production of this channel.

## C. The pass

| # | Statement | Type | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| C1 | "Thermopylae" = Hot Gates, after the sulphur hot springs at the cliff foot, still flowing today. | FACT | Hdt 7.176, 7.200; Britannica "Thermopylae" |
| C2 | In 480 the Malian Gulf reached the foot of Mount Kallidromo; the pass ran several kilometres between cliff and sea with three narrows (west, middle, east gates); Herodotus says the narrowest points were a single cart-track wide (7.176), the middle gate wider. Modern reconstructions put the middle gate at tens of metres. | FACT | Hdt 7.176; Britannica; Kraft et al., "The pass at Thermopylae, Greece", *J. Field Archaeology* 14 (1987) |
| C3 | Silt from the Spercheios and travertine deposits have moved the shoreline several kilometres seaward; the ancient shore is now farmland crossed by the Athens–Thessaloniki highway. | FACT | Kraft et al. 1987; Britannica "Thermopylae" |
| C4 | A wall built earlier by the Phocians against Thessalian raids crossed the middle gate; the Greeks rebuilt it and camped behind it. | FACT | Hdt 7.176, 7.208 |
| C5 | The Anopaea path left the Asopus gorge, climbed the ridge of Kallidromo and descended to the east gate near Alpeni; Leonidas posted the 1,000 Phocians on it. | FACT | Hdt 7.212–217 |
| C6 | The final stand was on a low hill ("kolonos") in the pass behind the wall (7.225). Spyridon Marinatos excavated the hill in 1939 and found large numbers of bronze arrowheads of Persian/Scythian trilobate type, taken as confirming the site. | FACT | Hdt 7.225; Marinatos, *Thermopylae: An Historical and Archaeological Guide* (1951); Britannica |

## D. Equipment and tactics

| # | Statement | Type | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| D1 | Hoplite kit c. 480: aspis (round shield about 80–100 cm, wood with bronze facing), dory (thrusting spear about 2–2.5 m, iron head, bronze butt-spike), short sword (xiphos; the curved kopis also attested), bronze helmet (Corinthian type common), bronze "bell" cuirass or layered linen corselet, bronze greaves. | FACT (as reconstructed from finds and vase painting) | Snodgrass, *Arms and Armour of the Greeks* (1967/1999); Sekunda, *Greek Hoplite 480–323 BC* (Osprey 2000) |
| D2 | Spartans wore crimson cloaks (phoinikis) and long hair; Herodotus' spy sees them combing their hair before the battle. | FACT | Xenophon, *Constitution of the Lacedaimonians* 11.3; Hdt 7.208–209 |
| D3 | The lambda (Λ) shield blazon is first attested in the Peloponnesian War era (Eupolis fr. 394, c. 420s); it is not evidenced for 480 and is NOT depicted. | FACT (absence) | Sekunda 2000; Anderson, *Military Theory and Practice in the Age of Xenophon* (1970) |
| D4 | Phalanx: close-order ranks (eight deep is typical in the sources), each man's shield covering his own left and the right of his neighbour on the left (Thucydides 5.71); spears levelled over the shield rim. In a confined space it cannot be outflanked. | FACT / INFERENCE (depth at Thermopylae not recorded) | Thuc. 5.71; Hdt 7.211; Hanson, *The Western Way of War* (1989) |
| D5 | Persian infantry (Hdt 7.61): soft felt tiaras, sleeved tunics of various colours, iron scale corselets "like fish scales", trousers, wicker shields (gerrha), quivers, short spears, large bows with reed arrows, daggers on the right thigh. Medes equipped the same way (7.62). | FACT | Hdt 7.61–62 |
| D6 | The Immortals: 10,000 picked Persian infantry under Hydarnes, kept at exactly 10,000 by immediate replacement, hence the name. | FACT | Hdt 7.83 |
| D7 | Herodotus' explicit explanation of the Persian failure: shorter spears, no room to use numbers, lack of skill against trained men (7.211). | FACT | Hdt 7.211 |

## E. The three days

| # | Statement | Type | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| E1 | Xerxes waited four days expecting the Greeks to withdraw; a mounted scout reported Spartans exercising and combing their hair; the exiled Spartan king Demaratus explained the custom. | FACT | Hdt 7.208–210 |
| E2 | Day 1: Medes and Cissians attacked with orders to take the Greeks alive and were repulsed with heavy loss, "making plain to everyone, and to the king himself, that there were many people but few men" (7.210); then the Immortals under Hydarnes, who did no better; the Spartans feigned flight, turned and cut down the pursuers (7.211); Xerxes leapt from his seat three times in fear for his army (7.212). | FACT | Hdt 7.210–212 |
| E3 | Dienekes, told that Persian arrows would hide the sun, replied that they would fight in the shade. | FACT (anecdote reported by Herodotus) | Hdt 7.226 |
| E4 | Day 2: assaults in relays; the Greeks fought by city in turns except the Phocians on the path; the Persians withdrew again. | FACT | Hdt 7.212 |
| E5 | Ephialtes son of Eurydemus, of Trachis (Malis), told Xerxes of the Anopaea path, hoping for a reward; Hydarnes led the Immortals out "about lamp-lighting time", marched all night through oak woods, and at dawn reached the Phocians, who heard them through the rustle of leaves; after a volley of arrows the Phocians withdrew to a hilltop to make a stand; the Persians ignored them and descended. | FACT | Hdt 7.213–218 |
| E6 | Day 3: deserters at night and lookouts at dawn warned the Greeks; a council; Herodotus says Leonidas sent the allies away (or they left), keeping the Spartans; the Thespians under Demophilus refused to go; the Thebans stayed, Herodotus says held as hostages, which modern historians doubt (Theban-hostile tradition). | FACT (Herodotus) / CONTESTED (the Thebans) | Hdt 7.219–222; Cartledge 2006; Lazenby 1993 |
| E7 | Herodotus' explanation: the Delphic oracle that Sparta would be sacked or lose a king (7.220). Modern explanation: a rearguard to cover the allies' withdrawal and keep the fleet informed (narrated as "modern historians point at the road"). | FACT (oracle as reported) / INFERENCE (rearguard) | Hdt 7.220; Lazenby 1993; Cartledge 2006 |
| E8 | Day 3 fighting: Xerxes attacked "about the time of the filling of the market" (mid-morning); the Greeks advanced beyond the wall to the wider part of the pass; most spears broke and they fought with swords; Leonidas fell; four times the sides fought over his body until the Greeks dragged it off; when Ephialtes' column arrived the survivors withdrew behind the wall to the hill and defended themselves "with swords, those who still had them, and with hands and teeth" until buried under missiles. | FACT | Hdt 7.223–225 |
| E9 | The Thebans separated and surrendered; most were spared but branded with the royal mark. | FACT (Herodotus) / CONTESTED (Theban sources differ) | Hdt 7.233 |
| E10 | Xerxes had Leonidas' head cut off and the body impaled; Herodotus notes Persians normally honoured brave enemies. | FACT | Hdt 7.238 |
| E11 | Casualties: Herodotus gives about 4,000 Greek dead (including helots) and 20,000 Persian; not narrated as precise figures. | FACT (Herodotus' figures) | Hdt 7.228, 8.24 |

## F. After and memory

| # | Statement | Type | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| F1 | Artemisium: the fleets fought on the same days; a storm off Magnesia had already wrecked a large part of the Persian fleet (Herodotus: at least 400 ships) and a squadron sent round Euboea was lost; three days of inconclusive fighting; on news of Thermopylae the Greek fleet withdrew by night. | FACT | Hdt 7.188–192, 8.1–21 |
| F2 | Athens was evacuated (Troezen, Aegina, Salamis); the Persians took and burned the Acropolis. | FACT | Hdt 8.41, 8.51–53 |
| F3 | Salamis (late September 480): the Greek fleet decisively defeated the Persian fleet in the strait; Xerxes returned to Asia leaving Mardonius, who was defeated at Plataea in 479 with the Spartans under Pausanias holding the decisive wing. | FACT | Hdt 8.83–96, 8.113–117, 9.19–70 |
| F4 | Significance debated: the delay was short and the pass fell; the stand's value lay in an orderly withdrawal, preserving the fleet, and its moral/political effect on Greek resistance. Narrated as a question with both sides. | CONTESTED / INFERENCE | Cartledge 2006; Lazenby 1993; Britannica |
| F5 | Simonides' epitaph for the Spartans: "Stranger, tell the Lacedaemonians that here we lie, obedient to their words/laws" (Hdt 7.228). | FACT | Hdt 7.228 |
| F6 | "Molon labe" ("come and take them") is not in Herodotus; it appears in Plutarch, *Sayings of the Spartans* 225D (c. AD 100). | FACT | Plutarch, *Apophthegmata Laconica* 225D |
| F7 | Leonidas' bones were brought to Sparta forty years after the battle, where a tomb and annual games (the Leonidea) existed. | FACT (Pausanias' report) | Pausanias 3.14.1 |
| F8 | Modern site: the 1955 Leonidas monument beside the highway; the Kolonos hill with a slab carrying the epitaph; the coast several kilometres away. | FACT | Britannica; Greek Ministry of Culture site listing |

## H. What is deliberately NOT shown or said

- No "300 alone" framing: the allies, the Thespians and the helots are named.
- No lambda blazons, capes-only costumes, monsters, exaggerated bodies, fictional armour, slow-motion gore.
- No modern text on generated images; all captions, maps and the epitaph card are deterministic graphics.
- No claim of exact Persian numbers, Leonidas' exact age, the phalanx's depth at the pass, or the content of the
  council on the third day; each is qualified in the script.
