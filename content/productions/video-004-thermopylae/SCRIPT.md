# Video #004 — Narration script (English)

Working title: **Three Days at the Hot Gates**
Alternative: *Thermopylae: The Pass That Held*

## S1 · HOOK

Late summer, 480 BC. A strip of shore between a mountain and the sea, in places no wider than a road.

Coming down the coast toward it: the largest invasion force Greece had ever seen.

Waiting inside it: about seven thousand Greeks, and three hundred of them from Sparta.

For two days, the pass held. Then a goat path through the mountains changed everything.

By the third afternoon, a king of Sparta was dead, and the men who had stayed with him lay around him on a low hill.

It is a harder story than the legend. And a better one.

This is Thermopylae.

## S2 · THE KING OF KINGS

Ten years earlier, a Persian army had landed at Marathon, north of Athens, and been thrown back into the sea.

The Great King, Darius, planned to return. He died first. His son Xerxes inherited the empire, the grudge, and the preparations.

For years the Persians stockpiled grain along the route, dug a canal through the neck of Mount Athos so the fleet could avoid the cape that had wrecked ships before, and bridged the Hellespont, the strait between Asia and Europe, with two floating roads built on hundreds of ships.

Herodotus says that when a storm broke the first bridges, Xerxes had the sea whipped.

How big was the army? Herodotus counted more than two million fighting men. Modern historians think somewhere between a hundred thousand and three hundred thousand, with a fleet of several hundred warships.

Even the low figure was larger than anything Greece had faced.

Heralds went ahead demanding earth and water: the sign of submission. Many northern cities gave it.

## S3 · THE GREEKS

The Greeks were not a nation. They were hundreds of quarrelling cities.

In 481, the ones that chose to resist met at Corinth and agreed, barely, to fight together.

Sparta would lead on land. Athens, which had just built two hundred warships with silver from its mines, would carry the fight at sea.

The plan was simple. Block the land road into central Greece at Thermopylae, and block the sea beside it at Artemisium, so the two Persian forces could not support each other.

But the timing was bad. Sparta was keeping the festival of the Carneia, and the Olympic truce was in force.

So Sparta sent an advance guard: one of its two kings, Leonidas, with three hundred full citizens, each chosen, Herodotus says, because he had a living son.

Leonidas was probably about sixty.

On the road north, the allies joined: men from Tegea and Mantinea, Corinth, Phlius, Mycenae, seven hundred from Thespiae, four hundred from Thebes, and the Phocians and Locrians, whose land lay just beyond the pass.

Roughly seven thousand men, with their helot servants. They were told the main army would follow after the festival.

## S4 · THE HOT GATES

Thermopylae means the Hot Gates. Sulphur springs still steam out of the cliff.

In 480 BC the sea came right up to the foot of the mountain. The pass ran for several kilometres between cliff and water, and at its narrowest points it was barely a cart's width.

Today the river silt has pushed the coast several kilometres away, and the ancient shoreline is a highway.

Across the middle of the pass stood an old wall the Phocians had built. The Greeks rebuilt it and camped behind it.

The men who held it were hoplites: citizen infantry.

Each carried a round shield almost a metre across, wood faced with bronze; a spear of two metres or more, with an iron head and a bronze spike at the butt; a short sword; a bronze helmet; body armour of bronze or layered linen; and greaves.

Spartans fought in crimson cloaks and wore their hair long. The famous lambda on the shield comes later; in 480 it did not exist.

They fought in a phalanx: ranks of men standing shoulder to shoulder, each shield covering the man on his left, spears levelled over the rim.

In the open, numbers could flank a phalanx. In a corridor a few metres wide, there was no flank.

The Persian infantry, as Herodotus describes them, wore felt caps, trousers and sleeved tunics, with scale armour under the cloth. They carried wicker shields, short spears, daggers, and above all bows.

Their best were the ten thousand the Greeks called the Immortals, because every man who fell was replaced at once.

They were brave, professional, and equipped for a different kind of war.

## S5 · THE FIRST DAY

Xerxes camped in the plain and waited four days, expecting the Greeks to run.

A horseman sent to look at them came back puzzled. The Spartans were exercising and combing their hair.

A Spartan exile at the king's court, Demaratus, explained: that is what they do when they are about to risk their lives.

On the fifth day Xerxes sent in the Medes and Cissians, with orders to take the Greeks alive.

They attacked the wall for hours. Short spears against long ones, wicker against bronze, in a space where only the front of the column could fight.

Herodotus says they made it clear to everyone, and to the king above all, that he had many men but few soldiers.

Then he sent the Immortals. They did no better.

The Spartans, fighting in front, would turn and feign flight, draw the pursuers into a rush, then wheel and cut them down.

Three times that day, Herodotus says, the king leapt up from his seat in fear for his army.

Before the battle, a Spartan named Dienekes was told that Persian arrows would hide the sun. Good, he said: then we will fight in the shade.

## S6 · THE PATH

The second day went the same way. The Persians attacked in relays; the Greeks rotated city by city, so fresh men always held the line.

And then, that evening, a local man came to the king's tent. His name was Ephialtes, from Trachis.

He knew a path. The Anopaea: a mountain track that climbed behind the pass, over the ridge, and came down beyond the Greek wall.

Leonidas knew it too. He had posted the thousand Phocians there to guard it.

At lamp-lighting time, Hydarnes led the Immortals out of the camp and up into the oak forest. They marched all night.

At first light the Phocians heard footsteps on fallen leaves, and looked up at thousands of men already on top of them.

Arrows came down. The Phocians pulled back to a hilltop to make a stand. The Persians ignored them and kept moving, down toward the pass.

## S7 · THE THIRD DAY

Before dawn, lookouts came running down the mountain. The Greeks knew.

They held a council. What was said, nobody recorded.

Herodotus says Leonidas sent the allies away and kept the Spartans. The seven hundred Thespians, under Demophilus, refused to leave. The four hundred Thebans also stayed.

Why stay? Herodotus points to an oracle: Sparta would lose a king, or lose the city.

Modern historians point at the road: somebody had to hold the pass long enough for the rest to get clear, and for the fleet at Artemisium to learn what had happened.

At mid-morning, Xerxes attacked from the front.

This time the Greeks did not wait behind the wall. They came out into the wider part of the pass, where more of them could fight at once, knowing the men on the path would soon arrive behind them.

The spears broke. They fought with swords.

Leonidas fell. Four times the two sides fought over his body, and four times the Greeks dragged it back.

Then the Immortals came down the mountain into the Greeks' rear.

The survivors withdrew behind the wall to a low mound and formed up on it, with swords, with hands, with teeth, Herodotus says.

The Persians did not close with them. They buried the hill in arrows.

In 1939, a Greek archaeologist, Spyridon Marinatos, dug into that mound. He found hundreds of Persian bronze arrowheads.

According to Herodotus, the Thebans surrendered and were branded. The Thespians died with the Spartans. Their names are rarely remembered.

Xerxes ordered Leonidas's head cut off and his body impaled, which Herodotus notes was unlike the Persians, who usually honoured brave enemies.

## S8 · AFTER

The same three days, the fleets had been fighting off Artemisium, in sight of the pass.

A storm had already wrecked many Persian ships. The battle was a draw. When the news came from Thermopylae, the Greek fleet withdrew in the night.

The Persians marched south. Athens emptied its people onto the islands, and the Acropolis burned.

Then, in the narrow strait of Salamis, the Greek fleet destroyed Xerxes's.

The king went home. The army he left behind was broken the next summer at Plataea, by a Greek line with the Spartans at its heart.

So, did Thermopylae matter? Three days did not stop the invasion. The pass fell.

But the retreat was orderly, the fleet survived, and every city in Greece now knew what resistance looked like.

## S9 · THE STONE

Simonides wrote the lines later carved at the pass: "Stranger, go tell the Spartans that here, obedient to their laws, we lie."

The famous answer, "Come and take them", does not appear in Herodotus. It was written down by Plutarch, five centuries later.

About forty years after the battle, Leonidas's bones were carried home to Sparta, and games were held there in his name every year.

The sea has gone. The gates are quiet.

But the low hill is still where it was, and the arrowheads were still in it.
