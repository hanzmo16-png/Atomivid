# Video #003 — Research packet: Lake Nyos (1986) and Lake Kivu

Every statement is tagged FACT (documented in the cited source), INFERENCE (reasonable interpretation,
stated as such in narration), or VISUAL RECONSTRUCTION (what we will depict; labelled as reconstruction
in the shot contract, never asserted as footage of the event). Each row was checked against published sources on
2026-09-30 (see VERIFICATION.md for claim / source / status / required qualification); the checklist column marks rows
whose wording was corrected by that check. A human re-read of the primary papers before recording remains required.

## A. The event

| # | Statement | Type | Source | Checked |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A1 | On 21 August 1986 a sudden release of carbon dioxide from Lake Nyos, north-west Cameroon, killed at least about 1,700 people; the most cited official figure is 1,746, with about 3,500 livestock (some sources say up to 8,000). | FACT (range) | Kling et al., Science 236:169–175 (1987); Britannica; NOAA NGDC event record | ☐ |
| A2 | The release happened in the evening; most accounts place it around 21:30 local time. | FACT | Wikipedia (citing Kling 1987 and survivor reports); SDSU "How volcanoes work" | ☐ |
| A3 | Survivors described a loud noise or rumble, a sulfur-like smell and a feeling of warmth before losing consciousness; some stayed unconscious up to about 36 hours. One survivor from Subum, Joseph Nkwain, described collapsing as he tried to reach his daughter and waking the next morning (about nine o'clock on the Friday, by his account). | FACT (testimony) | Baxter et al., BMJ 1989 (PMC1836556); Nkwain's recorded testimony as quoted by Wikipedia / Pan African Visions; NOT "around midnight" (corrected 2026-09-30) | ☑ |
| A4 | Pure CO2 is odourless; the reported smell and warmth are best explained by trace sulfur compounds and by the physiology of hypercapnia/hypoxia rather than by heat in the cloud. | INFERENCE | Baxter et al. 1989 discussion | ☐ |
| A5 | The gas cloud was heavier than air, flowed down the valleys, and affected people up to about 25 km from the lake; estimates give a cloud tens of metres thick moving at roughly 20–50 km/h. | FACT (estimates) | Kling 1987; SDSU; Wikipedia | ☐ |
| A6 | Villages named in the records include Nyos, Kam, Cha and Subum. | FACT | Kling 1987; Britannica | ☐ |
| A6b | Survival correlated with elevation: people in low-lying houses died while some only slightly higher up survived; the cloud thinned as it spread. | FACT (pattern reported by investigators) / INFERENCE (metres) | Kling 1987; Baxter et al. 1989 | ☐ |
| A6c | Cameroonian authorities and international teams (including USGS-affiliated scientists) investigated within weeks of the event. | FACT | Kling 1987 (field work Sept 1986) | ☐ |
| A7 | Estimates of CO2 released range from about 0.1 to 0.3 million tonnes; later work estimates the gas volume near 1 km³ at surface pressure. Use "hundreds of thousands of tonnes". | FACT (range) | Wikipedia summary of Kling 1987; Frontiers Earth Sci. 2021 (sound-speed estimate) | ☐ |
| A8 | After the event the lake's surface water had turned reddish-brown from iron brought up from depth and oxidized. | FACT | Kling 1987 | ☐ |
| A9 | A fountain or surge of water accompanied the release; wave/spray marks up to the order of 25 m on the shore and a higher surge on the southern shore are reported; a "100 m fountain" is quoted in secondary sources and must be narrated as "witnesses and shoreline damage suggest" | FACT (shore evidence) / INFERENCE (height) | Kling 1987; secondary summaries | ☐ |
| A10 | The trigger of the 1986 release is not known with certainty; hypotheses include a landslide or rockfall into the lake, cool rain-fed water sinking and destabilizing the stratification, or an internal seiche. | FACT (that it is uncertain) | Kling 1987; Kling et al. PNAS 2005 | ☐ |

## B. The lake and the mechanism

| # | Statement | Type | Source | Checked |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| B1 | Lake Nyos is a crater (maar) lake about 1 km across and around 200–210 m deep, on the Cameroon Volcanic Line. | FACT | Kling 1987; USGS | ☐ |
| B2 | CO2 of magmatic origin seeps through the lake bed into the deep water, where high pressure keeps it dissolved; the lake is stratified and the dense, gas-rich bottom water normally does not mix with the surface. | FACT | Kling 1987; Kling et al. 2005 | ☐ |
| B3 | Once gas-rich water is lifted enough for the pressure to drop below saturation, bubbles form, lift more water, and the process runs away: a limnic eruption. | FACT | Kling 1987; Kling 2005 | ☐ |
| B4 | The mechanism was first described for Lake Monoun (Cameroon), where a release on 15 August 1984 killed 37 people; poisoning and sabotage were suspected before the gas explanation. | FACT | Sigurdsson et al. 1987 (J. Volcanol. Geotherm. Res.); Frontiers Earth Sci. 2022 | ☐ |
| B5 | Only three lakes are known to hold this hazard at scale: Nyos, Monoun and Kivu. | FACT (as commonly stated in the literature) | Kling 2005; Nature 2021 | ☐ |

## C. The fix

| # | Statement | Type | Source | Checked |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| C0 | The degassing concept and first installation were led by a French engineering team (Michel Halbwachs and colleagues) with Cameroonian and international partners. | FACT | Kling et al. PNAS 2005; Halbwachs et al. 2004 (EOS) | ☐ |
| C1 | A degassing pipe was installed at Nyos in 2001 (about a 50 m fountain); two more pipes were added in late 2011 and early 2012 (EU-funded). Once primed, the fountain is self-sustaining: gas-rich water rises, degasses and lifts the column with no external power. Narrate as "a decade later", not "in 2011". | FACT | Kling et al. PNAS 2005; Kusakabe et al. 2019 (J. African Earth Sci.); Kusakabe et al. 2015 (CO2/3He); Wikipedia | ☑ |
| C2 | By 2019 the gas content had been brought down to a level where natural recharge is balanced by discharge through the pipes (steady state); monitoring continues. | FACT | Kusakabe et al. 2019, J. African Earth Sci. | ☐ |
| C3 | The lake's natural northern dam of weak pyroclastic rock was judged at risk of failure (flood modelled at about 17,000 m³/s reaching Nigeria, ~108 km away) and was reinforced by jet grouting and a concrete slab. | FACT | Lockwood et al. 1988 (USGS 70013723); 2005 expert warnings; later reinforcement reports | ☐ |
| C4 | Survivors (over 12,000) were moved to seven resettlement camps. A government return programme has been announced repeatedly but, as of 2025, return to the Nyos villages has not been completed and camp conditions are reported as poor, worsened by the Anglophone conflict since 2017. NOT narrated; the script makes no claim about return. | FACT (status) | VOA 2016; ReliefWeb; Pan African Visions 2025; Mimi Mefo Info 2025 | ☑ |

## D. Lake Kivu

| # | Statement | Type | Source | Checked |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| D1 | Lake Kivu (Rwanda / DR Congo) holds about 300 km³ of dissolved CO2 and about 60 km³ of methane; roughly 2 million people live around it. | FACT | Nature 2021 immersive; Schmid et al. | ☐ |
| D2 | Kivu's gas content is close to steady state and not currently increasing toward eruption; the risk is real but not imminent on current evidence. | FACT | PMC 2020 intercomparison study; RSC Environ. Sci. 2026 risk paper | ☐ |
| D3 | Methane is extracted from Kivu's deep water for power (KivuWatt); at current rates only a small fraction of the gas is removed. | FACT | Nature 2021; KivuWatt reporting | ☐ |
| D4 | Sediment cores show brown organic-rich layers that some authors read as past gas-release events (one proposed 3,500–5,000 years ago, at least two in the last 4,000 years); other authors find no evidence of a true limnic eruption in the last 12,000 years. Contested; narrated with both sides ("Some sediment studies suggest… others find no such sign"). | CONTESTED (narrated as such) | Earth Magazine "Ancient floods degassed Lake Kivu"; Earth-Science Reviews 2023 review (S0012825223002921); Wikipedia Lake Kivu | ☑ |
