# Video #003 — Narration script (English)

Working title: **The Lake That Held Its Breath**
Alternative: *The Night Lake Nyos Exhaled*

Target: 9–11 minutes at ~150 words per minute. Scene timings are the storyboard targets.

---

## S1 · HOOK (0:00–0:38)

On the night of August 21st, 1986, in the highlands of Cameroon, a valley went quiet.

No fire. No explosion. No storm.

By morning, more than seventeen hundred people were dead, along with thousands of cattle — in villages up to twenty-five kilometres from a small, beautiful lake.

The killer had no colour and no smell. For days, nobody could say what it was.

This is the story of a lake that held its breath for centuries — and the night it finally exhaled.

## S2 · THE LAKE (0:38–1:45)

Lake Nyos sits in a volcanic crater in north-west Cameroon, along a chain of volcanoes geologists call the Cameroon Volcanic Line.

It is not large. About a kilometre across. But it is deep — around two hundred metres — with steep crater walls and, on one side, a natural dam of loose volcanic rock.

From the rim it looks like any calm mountain lake. Farmers kept cattle on the slopes. Villages sat in the valleys below.

Nothing about the surface suggested what was dissolved underneath.

## S3 · THE MYSTERY (1:45–3:10)

The first reports were confusing. Survivors described a rumbling sound, then a smell like rotten eggs, then a strange warmth — and then nothing. Some woke a day and a half later, surrounded by silence.

One man from the village of Subum said he woke around midnight, unable to speak, and found that the people around him would not wake at all.

There were no burns. No wounds. Animals lay where they had stood.

Two years earlier, at Lake Monoun, a hundred kilometres to the south, thirty-seven people had died the same way. Poison was suspected. Sabotage was suspected. A volcanic eruption was suspected.

Scientists from Cameroon and from abroad arrived within weeks. They measured the air, the soil, the water. And they found the lake was still there. Only its colour had changed. The clear water had turned a rusty red.

## S4 · THE SCIENCE (3:10–5:15)

The answer was under the surface.

Beneath the crater, volcanic activity releases carbon dioxide. It seeps upward through the lake bed and dissolves into the deepest water.

Deep water is under pressure. Under pressure, water can hold enormous amounts of gas — like a sealed bottle of soda.

Lake Nyos is also stratified. The bottom layer, cold and heavy with gas, almost never mixes with the surface. So year after year, the gas kept accumulating. The lake was charging.

Then something disturbed it. Scientists still cannot say what. A landslide into the lake, or a slug of cold rainwater sinking through the layers — any of these could have lifted gas-rich water just far enough.

As it rose, the pressure dropped. Bubbles formed. Bubbles lift water. Lifted water releases more gas. In seconds, the process ran away.

The lake erupted — not lava, but gas. Shoreline damage suggests a surge of water tens of metres high. Then the carbon dioxide, heavier than air, settled back onto the lake and poured over the crater rim like an invisible flood, tens of metres thick, flowing downhill at up to fifty kilometres an hour.

It filled the valleys and pushed the air out of them. People asleep in low houses never woke; people on higher ground, sometimes only a few metres higher, survived. And then the cloud thinned, and left, the way it came, in silence.

The red colour was iron from the deep water, oxidizing at the surface — the lake's own evidence of what it had done.

## S5 · THE FIX (5:15–7:10)

The disturbing part was not the eruption. It was the recharge.

Measurements showed the gas was building up again. Unless something changed, Nyos would exhale a second time.

The solution, proposed by a French-led engineering team, was almost absurdly simple: a pipe.

In 2001, engineers lowered a pipe from a raft to the bottom of the lake and pumped water up it once. After that, no pump was needed. Gas-rich water rising in the pipe begins to fizz; the bubbles lift the column; the column lifts more water.

A fountain shot into the air — a controlled, permanent exhale, driven by the lake's own pressure.

Two more pipes followed in 2011. By 2019, the gas leaving through the pipes balanced the gas seeping in. Nyos had reached a steady state.

The weak natural dam was another problem: engineers feared its collapse could send a flood a hundred kilometres downstream and trigger a larger gas release. It has since been reinforced with grouting and a concrete cap.

The lake is monitored. The fountain still runs.

## S6 · KIVU (7:10–9:05)

Nyos and Monoun are small. There is a third lake.

Lake Kivu, on the border of Rwanda and the Democratic Republic of the Congo, is roughly three thousand times bigger.

Its deep water holds around three hundred cubic kilometres of dissolved carbon dioxide — and sixty cubic kilometres of methane.

Around two million people live on its shores.

Here, the science is measured carefully, and the current answer is measured too: the gas is close to a steady state, and there is no sign that an eruption is imminent.

Rwanda extracts methane from the deep water to generate electricity — a slow drawdown of the pressure, but at present rates only a small fraction of the gas.

Sediment studies suggest Kivu may have erupted thousands of years ago. Nobody wants to learn what that looked like with cities on the shore.

## S7 · ENDING (9:05–10:05)

Lake Nyos taught the world that a lake can behave like a volcano — quietly, invisibly, and all at once.

It also taught something else: that the same physics that made it deadly could be used to make it safe. A pipe, a fountain, and the patience to keep measuring.

Today, on a calm day in the Cameroon highlands, you can stand on the crater rim and watch a jet of white water rise from the middle of the lake.

It is the lake breathing out — slowly, on purpose, so that it never has to do it all at once again.
